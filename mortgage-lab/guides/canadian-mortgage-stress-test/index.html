Canadian mortgage stress test: qualifying rate vs. actual payment rate

When shopping for a Canadian mortgage, you may see two very different rates: the rate you might actually pay, and the higher rate used to test borrowing capacity. They serve different purposes.

What is the qualifying rate?

For most newly underwritten uninsured mortgages at federally regulated lenders, OSFI's current minimum qualifying rate is the greater of your contract rate plus 2 percentage points, or 5.25%. A lender uses the qualifying rate alongside other underwriting information to assess whether a borrower can handle financial stress. It is not automatically the interest rate charged on the mortgage.

Official source: OSFI — Minimum qualifying rate for uninsured mortgages.

A simple illustrative example

If a lender offers an interest rate of 4.5%, adding 2 percentage points gives 6.5%. Because 6.5% is above 5.25%, that would be the stress-test benchmark in the example. Monthly payments at the actual contract rate and qualifying-rate calculations are different concepts, so do not confuse the benchmark with a lender's payment quote.

Approval depends on more than this comparison: income, existing debt, credit, property, lender policies and other costs may all be relevant. Mortgage Lab cannot issue a preapproval or determine lending eligibility.

Does this rule apply to every mortgage?

No. Uninsured and insured mortgages may have different governing requirements, and certain renewals or switches can be treated differently. The appropriate rule depends on the lender and transaction. Check directly with a regulated lender or qualified mortgage professional.

How to use a higher-rate scenario responsibly

  • Enter the estimated purchase price and down payment in Mortgage Lab.
  • Compare the same mortgage at the expected rate and a higher rate, for example a 2 percentage-point increase.
  • Include taxes, heating, maintenance, condo fees and other recurring costs in the budget.
  • Check what an additional principal payment would change, but review prepayment restrictions and penalties in the actual mortgage contract.
  • Remember that interest rates and terms may differ at renewal; the calculator's estimate cannot predict future rates.

Questions worth asking your lender

  • What is the actual offered contract rate, term and amortization?
  • Which qualifying rate and debt-service calculations are being used?
  • How would a variable-rate mortgage behave if rates change?
  • What are the prepayment limits and any penalties?
  • What is the expected payment and balance at the end of the mortgage term?
